What a forward deployed engineer actually does

The title came out of Palantir, and most AI startups now use it for the person who embeds with a customer, understands their data and workflows, and makes the product work in that environment. Day to day that means scoping the deployment, writing integration code and configuration, running workshops with the customer's team, feeding what they learn back to product, and owning the outcome until the customer is live and getting value.

A deployment strategist is the less code-heavy version of the same seat. They own the customer relationship and the implementation plan, lean on engineering for the hard technical lifts, and are usually measured on time to value and expansion. Many startups hire one of each per enterprise account; early on, one person often covers both.

Rule of thumb: if your product needs more than a week of hands-on work before an enterprise customer sees value, you need a forward deployed function, not a support queue.

When to make this hire

  • You have signed, or are about to sign, your first two or three enterprise customers and the founders are personally running the deployments.
  • Core engineers are spending more than a quarter of their time on customer-specific work.
  • Deals are stalling in pilot because nobody owns getting the customer live.
  • You sell into a domain (finance, healthcare, insurance, defense, legal) where the customer's data and process are messy and specific.

What strong candidates look like

On paper

  • Prior FDE, deployment strategist, solutions engineer, implementation lead or technical consultant titles at a company with real enterprise customers.
  • Evidence of shipping code in production, even if they were not a full-time software engineer. Python and SQL are the common denominators; the rest depends on your stack.
  • A track record in your customer's industry, or in a similarly messy one. Someone who has deployed into a hospital system will adapt to a bank faster than a pure product engineer will.
  • Tenure that shows they stayed through at least one full deployment cycle, ideally through a renewal or expansion.

In the room

  • They can explain a past deployment from kickoff to go-live, including what went wrong and what they changed.
  • They talk about the customer's business problem before they talk about the technology.
  • They are comfortable saying no to a customer and explaining why, which protects your roadmap.
  • They ask you how success is measured for the role. The best ones have been burned by vague ownership before.

How to interview for the role

  1. Deployment walk-through (45 min). Pick one past deployment and go deep. Who was the champion, what was the data situation, where did it slip, how did they recover.
  2. Practical exercise (take-home or live). Give them a realistic, anonymized customer scenario with sample data and ask for a deployment plan plus a small piece of working code or a data transformation. Judge the plan as much as the code.
  3. Customer simulation (30 min). A founder or early customer plays a skeptical buyer. Watch how they handle pushback and scope creep.
  4. Engineering pairing (45 min). Not a leetcode screen. Pair on something that resembles your actual integration work.

Mistakes that cost founders months

  • Hiring a pure software engineer and hoping they will enjoy customer work. Most will not, and they will leave.
  • Hiring a consultant who cannot code and expecting them to unblock themselves. They will pull your engineers back into every deployment.
  • Writing the job description as a wish list. The role is already hard to fill; asking for ten years of experience in a field that is three years old shrinks the pool to zero.
  • Running a slow process. FDE candidates with real experience get multiple offers. Two weeks from first call to offer is competitive; five is not.
  • Leaving comp vague. These candidates compare offers from well-funded AI companies and know what the market pays.

Common questions

What is the difference between a forward deployed engineer and a solutions engineer?

A solutions engineer usually supports the sales cycle: demos, proofs of concept, technical questions before the deal closes. A forward deployed engineer owns what happens after the signature: integration, configuration and getting the customer live. Some startups combine the two early on, but the skills and incentives are different.

Do forward deployed engineers need to be strong coders?

They need to be able to write production-quality code in your integration layer, usually Python and SQL plus whatever your product exposes. They do not need to be your strongest systems engineer. Judgment with customers matters as much as raw engineering depth.

Where are most FDE candidates based?

San Francisco and New York hold most of the experienced pool, because that is where the companies that pioneered the role are. Many are open to relocating between the two. Fully remote FDE roles are rare because the job involves time on site with customers.

How long does it take to hire a forward deployed engineer?

With a focused search and a tight interview loop, three to five weeks from kickoff to accepted offer is realistic. Slow scheduling is the most common reason searches run longer.

Should my first forward deployed hire be an engineer or a strategist?

If deployments are blocked on code, hire the engineer. If deployments are blocked on customer alignment, scoping and change management, hire the strategist. If both, hire the engineer first; strategists are easier to find and ramp.
